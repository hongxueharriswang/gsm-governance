# A Tutorial on the GSM-J Computational Theory

**Learning the computational theory of justice-centered multi-level governance for human flourishing under bounded non-exploitation**

---

## Preface: what this tutorial is for

This tutorial teaches the theory itself — not the library, not the paper's structure, not the constitutional instrument, but the **idea**. It is organized as thirteen lessons in five parts, each building on the last. If you read it end to end, you will be able to:

- State what the theory claims and what it does not claim.
- Explain each of the three innovations the theory calls irreducible, and why they are irreducible.
- Read the formal apparatus — state space, interaction matrix, dynamics, welfare — as an argument rather than an equation list.
- Recognize the eight hypotheses the theory makes, and what would falsify each.
- Distinguish the theory's *conditional* status from a normative doctrine and from a technical simulation.
- Use the theory as a diagnostic for policy design, a vocabulary for inter-jurisdictional disputes, and a generator of empirical questions.

The tutorial assumes you have seen the paper's title and abstract. It does not assume you have read the whole paper, and it is written so that you can read the paper afterward with the ideas already in place. Where the tutorial departs from the paper's language, it does so deliberately to teach a concept. Where it introduces notation, it introduces the notation as a way of stating something you already understand in plain English.

Throughout, examples use a **stylized Nordic democracy** — three jurisdictions in a parent-child chain, high-capability but with moderate recognition and intergenerational shortfalls. The example comes from §6 of the paper. It is illustrative, not empirical; the tutorial uses it for the same reason the paper does, which is to have a concrete object to point at while explaining abstract ideas.

The parts are:

- **Part I** — the problem the theory solves.
- **Part II** — the three innovations, in plain terms.
- **Part III** — the formalism that makes the innovations precise.
- **Part IV** — what the theory predicts and what kind of theory it is.
- **Part V** — how to work with it.

Each part has lessons; each lesson has a takeaway.

# Part I — The Problem

## Lesson 1: Why governance needs a theory of justice

Ask a well-intentioned governance researcher what justice is and you will get one of three answers. Either justice is a **constraint** — a side-constraint that public decisions must not violate (do not steal, do not deceive, do not coercively extract) — or justice is an **outcome** — a distribution of resources or capabilities that a well-ordered society should achieve — or justice is a **procedure** — a fair process that yields legitimate decisions, whatever they are.

Each of these answers is correct as far as it goes, and each fails in the same way when governance is multi-level. A constraint does not tell you what to do when the constraint is satisfied but the outcome is unjust. An outcome does not tell you who is responsible for reaching it when there are six levels of government. A procedure does not tell you what to do when every level follows the procedure and the result is worse than no procedure at all.

The GSM-J theory begins with the observation that **governance is unavoidably multi-level**, and that this fact creates problems that single-level theories of justice cannot see. A higher level of government can extract resources from a lower one, externalize environmental harm across boundaries, or issue regulations whose costs fall on jurisdictions that had no voice in making them. A present generation can deplete the capital on which future generations depend. A powerful state can enter into agreements that are formally voluntary and substantively coercive.

None of these is captured by a theory that treats the nation-state as the unit of analysis. All of them are **jurisdictional** in character: the failure is not between persons, but between jurisdictions — levels, territories, generations, communities — whose relationship is structured by institutions and not by contract.

### What the theory is trying to do

The theory's claim is that if you want to reason systematically about justice in a multi-level system, you need a theory that:

1. **Represents the multi-level structure explicitly.** Six levels, coupled jurisdictions, vertical and horizontal interaction.
2. **Treats justice as a governance capability**, not just a constraint or an outcome. Justice is something a jurisdiction can be better or worse at, something that can be built and lost, something that co-evolves with the other things governance does.
3. **Formalizes the conditions under which jurisdictions can legitimately impose costs on one another**, and makes those conditions testable.
4. **Generates hypotheses that could be false.** A theory of justice that cannot fail is not a theory; it is a doctrine.

The first three points define the shape of the theory. The fourth defines the standard the theory holds itself to.

### Takeaway

A theory of justice for multi-level governance must represent the multi-level structure, treat justice as a governance capability, formalize the conditions of legitimate cross-jurisdictional cost-imposition, and generate falsifiable hypotheses. The GSM-J theory does these four things.

## Lesson 2: The justice gap

Here is the observation that motivates the entire theory, and it is the first of the three innovations the theory calls irreducible.

### The observation

**Bounded non-exploitation is necessary but insufficient for justice.**

Consider what it means for a jurisdiction not to exploit another. Following the theory's §2.2, exploitation is a transfer of advantage from one jurisdiction to another without adequate compensation. Bounded non-exploitation says: a jurisdiction may impose costs on another, but not beyond a tolerance threshold, and any cost above the threshold must be compensated.

This is a real and important condition. It rules out the most obvious form of injustice — the strong extracting from the weak without redress. If every jurisdiction respected this condition, the world would be considerably more just than it is.

But **satisfying it perfectly leaves five forms of injustice intact**:

1. **Distributive injustice below the exploitation threshold.** A jurisdiction may be much poorer than another without the richer having extracted anything from it. The poverty may be historical, structural, or accidental. It may violate a floor without violating the non-exploitation constraint.

2. **Procedural exclusion without extraction.** A jurisdiction may be excluded from decisions that affect it without any measurable cost being imposed. It is a matter of standing, not extraction. The exclusion is real, and it is a harm, and the exploitation index does not see it.

3. **Recognition injustice without resource harm.** A community may be denied standing, forced to assimilate, or subjected to domination without any resource transfer in either direction. The Roma population example from Lesson 1 is this. No extraction; still unjust.

4. **Corrective neglect of ended harm.** A wrong may have been done long ago and not yet remedied. Nothing is being extracted now, so the exploitation index is silent. The victims are still harmed.

5. **Intergenerational depletion through voluntary transactions.** A present generation may sell off natural capital, deplete fiscal reserves, or erode institutional trust — all through transactions the present generation freely enters into, with no current victim to extract from. The future is harmed, but no present jurisdiction can point to exploitation.

### Why this is called a "gap"

The five forms of injustice are not marginal. They are the **normal forms of structural injustice in multi-level systems**: the harm that operates below the threshold of detectable exploitation, through background institutions and accumulated advantage.

They are a gap in the sense that a theory which only addresses exploitation would miss them all. A system can be perfectly non-exploitative and still fail in every one of the five ways. That is the gap.

### The move the theory makes

The theory's response to the gap is the second innovation: **justice must be modeled as a governance capability, not merely a constraint on exploitation**. If bounded non-exploitation were the whole theory, the theory would be a theory of extraction. Since it is not, the theory needs to represent justice as a thing a jurisdiction can build and lose, across dimensions, at every level.

The third innovation — the **constraint architecture** — is what prevents the capability from collapsing back into an aggregate index. Each of the four constraints targets a specific failure that the others miss. We will see this in Lesson 4.

### Takeaway

Bounded non-exploitation is necessary but insufficient for justice, because the normal forms of structural injustice operate below the threshold of detectable extraction. The justice gap is the theory's reason for existing: it says that any theory that stops at non-exploitation is a theory of extraction masquerading as a theory of justice.

# Part II — The Three Innovations

## Lesson 3: Justice as a five-dimensional capability

The theory's second innovation is to model justice not as one thing but as **five dimensions held simultaneously**:

- **Distributive justice** — fair share of resources and capabilities.
- **Procedural justice** — voice, participation, and due process.
- **Recognition justice** — non-domination, non-exclusion, non-assimilation.
- **Corrective justice** — remedy for ended harm.
- **Intergenerational justice** — preservation of future-oriented capital.

### Why five, and why these five

The five dimensions are not a list. They are the set of distinct **kinds of harm** that the justice gap identifies. Each dimension catches a form of injustice that the others miss:

- **Distributive justice** catches the harm of inadequate resources — poverty, capability deprivation, unfair shares. It is what most people think of first when they think of justice.
- **Procedural justice** catches the harm of being excluded from decisions that affect you. A jurisdiction may receive a fair share without having a voice in what the share is for.
- **Recognition justice** catches the harm of domination and exclusion — being treated as less than a peer. A jurisdiction may have both fair resources and fair procedures and still be subjected to assimilation or denial of standing. This is the dimension that distinguishes the theory from most governance frameworks.
- **Corrective justice** catches the harm of past wrongs that remain unremedied. A distribution may be fair in the present and still rest on an unjust foundation.
- **Intergenerational justice** catches the harm done to people who do not yet exist and cannot speak for themselves. A decision that is fair to all present parties may deplete what belongs to the future.

Each dimension is **necessary**. Drop any one and the theory cannot see the kind of harm that dimension is for. This is what the theory means when it says the dimensions are irreducible: they are not different aspects of a single underlying quantity; they are different quantities.

### The scalar aggregate and why it does not collapse the dimensions

The theory's §3.3.1 addresses a question that arises immediately: if the dimensions are irreducible, why is there a scalar aggregate `J = Σ w_d · J_d`?

The answer is that the aggregate is a **reporting device, not a claim of substitutability**. It is used for summary comparison and for welfare calculation. It is *never* used inside a constraint check. The constraint architecture (Lesson 4) operates on the disaggregated vector and prevents the aggregate from licensing neglect of a single dimension.

This distinction matters because a naive reading of the aggregate would invite the inference that a jurisdiction can trade recognition for distributive gains. In GSM-J, it cannot. The floor constraint (C2) and the vertical alignment constraint (C4) both target the dimensions separately.

### The four roles of justice

Justice plays four roles in the theory, and it is worth naming them because a reader of the paper will encounter all four:

1. **A normative evaluative principle** — what counts as a fair distribution, procedure, recognition, remedy, and intergenerational transfer. In this role justice is asserted and contested, not measured.
2. **A measurable governance capability** — something a jurisdiction can be assessed on, compared on, and tracked over time.
3. **A dynamic systems variable** — something that evolves through interaction, alignment pressure, exploitation drag, and intervention.
4. **The organizing principle** — what gives the multi-level system its normative direction. Justice is not merely *a* capability among the six; it is the capability that tells the system what the other five are for.

The paper's §2.4 calls the first three the "ontological status" of justice, and it is the most important passage for a reader who wants to know what the theory is *doing* with the concept. Justice is not reduced to measurement (which would make the theory positivist) and not left as an unmeasurable ideal (which would make it unfalsifiable). It is held in three roles simultaneously, and the theory explicitly states the argument: "a principle without a measure is unenforceable, a measure without a dynamic is inert, and a dynamic without a principle has no direction."

### Recognition justice as the distinguishing dimension

The theory makes a specific claim about recognition justice that is worth understanding carefully: recognition captures forms of domination and exclusion that **neither distributive equality nor bounded non-exploitation can detect**. A jurisdiction may transfer resources fairly and impose no measurable cost on another while still denying standing, distorting voice, or demanding assimilation. None of these harms appears in an exploitation index. None of them appears in a distributive analysis.

Recognition is the dimension through which the theory reaches the part of the justice gap that extraction-based frameworks cannot see. It is not one dimension among five equal peers; it is the dimension that most clearly marks the theory's difference from welfare economics and from capability frameworks.

### Takeaway

Justice is a five-dimensional capability held simultaneously. The dimensions are irreducible because each catches a distinct kind of harm that the others miss. The scalar aggregate is for reporting only; the constraints never use it. Recognition justice is the dimension that most clearly distinguishes the theory from extraction-based and welfare-based frameworks.

## Lesson 4: The constraint architecture

The third innovation is the four constraints. They are the mechanism that prevents the five-dimensional capability from collapsing into an aggregate, and they are what makes the theory's claims about justice non-trivial.

### C1 — Bounded non-exploitation

**What it says.** For every directed pair of jurisdictions `(i, j)` and every time `t`, either `E_ij(t) ≤ τ_j` (exploitation at or below jurisdiction `j`'s tolerance threshold) or `Comp_ij(t) ≥ E_ij(t)` (exploitation is fully compensated).

**What it catches.** The most obvious form of injustice: a jurisdiction extracting from another without redress.

**What it misses.** All five forms of injustice in the justice gap. C1 alone is not a theory of justice.

### C2 — Dimension-specific justice floor

**What it says.** For every jurisdiction, every level, and every dimension, `J^d(t) ≥ J_min^d`. No dimension may fall below its floor.

**What it catches.** The accumulation of disadvantage below the exploitation threshold. A jurisdiction may be perfectly non-exploitative and still fail to meet its floor.

**Why dimension-specific and not aggregate.** An aggregate floor would let a high-performing dimension offset a failing one. The theory's claim is that no dimension can be traded against the aggregate — the floor for recognition is the floor for recognition, regardless of how distributive is doing.

### C3 — Vulnerability-sensitive non-exploitation

**What it says.** If a jurisdiction is below the aggregate floor, the tolerance threshold is reduced: `τ_j^vuln = φ · τ_j` with `φ ∈ [0, 1]`. Exploitation that would be admissible against a high-capability jurisdiction is not admissible against a vulnerable one.

**What it catches.** The failure of C1 to differentiate between exploitation of a robust and exploitation of a vulnerable party. A uniform threshold is systematically too generous to those who exploit the vulnerable.

**Why this matters.** C3 is not a special case; it is a **condition of the threshold**. The theory treats vulnerability as a reason for heightened rather than relaxed protection, and C3 is how this shows up in the constraint architecture.

### C4 — Dimension-wise vertical alignment

**What it says.** For each dimension `d` separately, `J_child^d ≥ J_parent^d − φ_d`. A child jurisdiction may fall below its parent by at most the tolerance margin, dimension by dimension.

**What it catches.** The failure of C1 and C2 to prevent a parent from achieving justice at the child's expense — or from claiming aggregate performance while leaving the child below in a specific dimension.

**Why absolute and not proportional.** A proportional rule (`J_child ≥ φ · J_parent`) would let a child fall far below a high-performing parent in absolute terms. The theory's rule caps the absolute regression, so that a high-capability parent cannot justify a large shortfall in the child.

### How the four constraints fit together

Each constraint does something the others do not:

- **C1** rules out extraction without compensation.
- **C2** rules out injustice below the extraction threshold.
- **C3** rules out uniform thresholds that systematically fail the vulnerable.
- **C4** rules out cross-level regressions that aggregate measures would miss.

No single constraint is sufficient. No single constraint is redundant. The four together are what makes the theory's claims about justice non-trivial.

### Takeaway

The four constraints are the mechanism that prevents the five-dimensional capability from collapsing into an aggregate. Each constraint targets a specific failure the others miss. The architecture is a **system** of constraints, not a list of rules.

# Part III — The Formalism

## Lesson 5: The state space

The theory represents each jurisdiction with a state vector. The vector has six entries: five **capabilities** and one **justice cluster**.

### The five capabilities

The capabilities are inherited from the General Governance Success Model, a prior model the theory extends. They are:

- **Accountability (A)** — the answerability of power-holders to affected parties.
- **Institutional competence (C)** — the capacity to design and deliver public goods.
- **Social cohesion (S)** — trust, shared norms, and collective efficacy.
- **Strategic continuity (T)** — consistency of long-horizon commitments.
- **Adaptive learning (L)** — the capacity to update from feedback and error.

Each is a scalar in `[0, 1]`.

### The justice cluster

The justice cluster is a five-vector, one entry per dimension. Each entry is a scalar in `[0, 1]`. The state vector is therefore six values, five of which are capabilities and one of which is a five-vector.

**Why five capabilities and not more.** The theory inherits them from the GSM and does not claim they are exhaustive. They are the capabilities that prior research has found to be empirically distinguishable and consequential. The theory's contribution is not a new list of capabilities; it is the addition of justice as a sixth cluster and the argument that justice is the one that gives the others direction.

**Why the capabilities co-evolve with justice.** §4.2 of the paper states that justice is neither epiphenomenal nor autonomous, but **co-constituted** with the other capabilities. This is a substantive theoretical claim: a jurisdiction that builds accountability tends to build justice, and vice versa. The library models this as a drift-toward-mean effect (a placeholder, not a substantive model), but the theoretical commitment is that the coupling exists.

### Levels

The theory models six levels:

1. Household / individual
2. Community
3. Subnational
4. National
5. Regional
6. Global

Each level has a representative unit. The levels are coupled through parent-child relationships and through horizontal neighbors.

**Why these six and not fewer.** The theory's argument is that justice is realized or violated at every one of these levels, and that the interactions between levels are what make multi-level governance hard. Collapsing to fewer levels would lose the failures the theory is designed to catch: a household subjected to assimilation pressure, a community subjected to exclusion by a municipality, a present generation depleting a global commons.

**Why not more levels.** More levels would add resolution without adding kinds of failure. The six are the resolution at which the theory's claims are testable.

### Takeaway

The state space is six capabilities plus a five-dimensional justice cluster, at six levels. The capabilities are inherited; the justice cluster is the theory's addition. The levels are the resolution at which the theory's claims are testable.

## Lesson 6: The interaction matrix and the contested pair

The five justice dimensions do not develop in isolation. Procedural voice and recognition reinforce each other; distributive capacity enables corrective remedy; but present-oriented distribution displaces future investment. The theory models these interactions with a **signed interaction matrix** `M_J`.

### The basic idea

`M_J` is a 5×5 matrix whose entry `m_de` captures how much a change in dimension `e` affects the marginal return to building dimension `d`. A positive entry means the dimensions are synergistic; a negative entry means they compete.

The matrix enters the dynamics through a coupling term `C_J^d(J)` that sums the interaction effects across dimensions, with a diminishing-returns factor `(1 - J^d)` so that near-saturation, the marginal coupling shrinks.

### The decomposition

The theory's §3.3.2 makes a specific move: the matrix is decomposed into a **non-negative synergy component** `M_J^+` and a **non-positive tension component** `M_J^-`, with `M_J = M_J^+ + M_J^-` and `m_de = m_de^+ + m_de^-`.

**Why decompose.** Because a net coefficient near zero can mean one of two very different things:

- The pair genuinely has no effect.
- The pair has a large positive effect and a large negative effect that cancel.

These two cases have different theoretical implications. If a pair has no effect, the theory should not propose a mechanism for it. If a pair has two offsetting effects, the theory should propose both mechanisms and treat the net as an empirical question.

### The contested pair

The theory identifies exactly one **contested pair**: `(I, P)`, intergenerational → procedural. The synergy component is that **intergenerational concern extends procedural inclusion** — future constituencies are given standing in present deliberation. The tension component is that **intergenerational obligation constrains present procedural choice** — commitments to successors restrict what present majorities may decide.

Both mechanisms are real. Which one dominates is an empirical question, not a theoretical one. The contested pair is where the theory is least committal and where estimation is most informative.

### The other pairs

Beyond the one contested pair, the theory commits to:

- **Seven synergy-only pairs** (single-component, synergy specified, tension fixed at zero). Examples: procedural ↔ recognition, distributive ↔ procedural, corrective ↔ recognition.
- **Four tension-only pairs** (single-component, tension specified, synergy fixed at zero). Examples: intergenerational → distributive, corrective → distributive, procedural → intergenerational.

Together: **twelve committed pairs and eight uncommitted pairs**. The twelve are where the theory makes commitments; the eight are where it is silent.

### Why this matters for testing

The contested/single-component distinction changes what a null result means:

- For a **single-component pair**, a net near zero is evidence against the proposed mechanism. The theory put itself at risk and lost.
- For a **contested pair**, a net near zero is consistent with two offsetting mechanisms and is uninformative about either.

Hypothesis H6 (Lesson 9) is a hypothesis about this difference. It is one of the theory's distinctive empirical claims.

### Takeaway

The interaction matrix is decomposed into synergy and tension components so that a pair carrying both can be distinguished from a pair carrying one. The contested pair `(I, P)` is where the theory is least committal; the single-component pairs are where the theory is at risk. The distinction is what makes the theory's claims about interactions testable.

## Lesson 7: The dynamics

The theory's dynamics specify how the state evolves from one period to the next. There are three dynamics to understand: justice, capabilities, and accountability.

### Justice dynamics

For each dimension `d` and each level `l`:

```
J^d(t+1) = J^d(t) + α_d · [ C_J^d(J) + β_d · (J_parent^d - J^d) - γ_d · E^d ]
```

Three terms inside the bracket:

1. **`C_J^d(J)`** — endogenous interaction. The coupling term from the interaction matrix. Positive when the jurisdiction's other dimensions reinforce `d`; negative when they compete.
2. **`β_d · (J_parent^d - J^d)`** — vertical alignment pressure. The jurisdiction is pulled toward its parent's level in this dimension, at rate `β_d`. This is the **dynamic counterpart to C4**: C4 is the constraint, `β` is the force.
3. **`γ_d · E^d`** — exploitation drag. Exploitation in dimension `d` reduces the jurisdiction's capacity to build justice in `d`. The drag is the dynamic consequence of being exploited.

**Why these three terms.** The three terms correspond to the three mechanisms the theory identifies as shaping justice trajectories: endogenous interaction (the dimensions reinforce or displace each other), vertical alignment (the levels pull on each other), and exploitation (extraction sets the jurisdiction back). Nothing else enters the dynamics; the theory is deliberately parsimonious.

**Why `β` matters.** Without `β`, the vertical alignment constraint (C4) would be a post-hoc check: the constraint would catch violations but not shape trajectories. With `β`, alignment is a **force** that pulls the system toward compliance. The paper's §4.1 explicitly includes the `β` term for this reason.

### Capability dynamics

The capabilities co-evolve with justice through a coupling term. The paper's §4.2 does not fix the form; the theory commits to the *existence* of co-evolution, not to a specific functional form. The library's `capability_step` uses a first-order drift toward mean justice as a placeholder, but a research study would substitute its own functional form.

**Why the theory does not fix the form.** Because the co-evolution is a claim about direction, not magnitude. A jurisdiction that improves its justice tends to improve its capabilities; a jurisdiction that loses justice tends to lose capabilities. The precise rate is an empirical question.

### Accountability dynamics

For each dimension `d`:

```
A^d(t+1) = A^d(t) + λ_pd · (J_child^d - J_parent^d)
```

A parent's accountability in dimension `d` *decreases* when the child falls below the parent (the child's justice minus the parent's is negative), and *increases* when the child rises above. This is the mechanism that ensures a parent cannot claim aggregate accountability while neglecting a specific dimension.

**Why dimension-specific.** A single accountability scalar would let a parent claim to be "accountable overall" while leaving one dimension far below. The dimension-specific accountability term targets each dimension separately.

### Takeaway

Three dynamics: justice (interaction + alignment + drag), capabilities (co-evolution with justice), accountability (dimension-specific cross-level). The dynamics are deliberately parsimonious; each term corresponds to a mechanism the theory identifies as important.

## Lesson 8: Welfare

The theory needs a way to evaluate whether one state of the world is better than another. §4.3 provides a **dimension-specific prioritarian welfare function**:

```
W = Σ_l Σ_i Σ_d w_d · [ γ_d · J^d - δ_d · (1 - J^d)² ]
```

Two things are happening:

1. **The dimensions are weighted** (`w_d`) and the level is rewarded (`γ_d · J^d`). A jurisdiction with high justice in dimension `d` contributes positively to welfare.
2. **The shortfall is penalized quadratically** (`δ_d · (1 - J^d)²`). A jurisdiction far from full justice in dimension `d` contributes negatively, and the penalty grows with the square of the shortfall.

### Why quadratic shortfall

Three properties the theory needs:

- **Monotone in each dimension.** More justice is always better than less, other things equal.
- **Convex in shortfall.** The worst off receive disproportionate weight, which is the prioritarian commitment. A jurisdiction at 0.1 contributes more to the penalty than a jurisdiction at 0.9 does, per unit of shortfall.
- **Separable across dimensions.** The interaction structure is carried by the dynamics, not double-counted in welfare. This keeps the welfare function's form and the dynamics' form doing different jobs.

The quadratic form is not a claim that the moral loss from injustice is literally quadratic. It is a **prioritarian device** chosen for tractability and for the three properties above. A user who prefers a linear, exponential, or lexicographic form can substitute it; the architecture does not depend on the specific functional form.

### Prioritarian condition

The welfare function is **prioritarian** when `δ_d > γ_d`. In that case, improvements to the worst off receive greater weight than improvements to the best off. The theory does not require prioritarianism — it requires that the commitment be explicit. A user who rejects prioritarianism can set `δ_d = γ_d` and get an egalitarian welfare function; a user who rejects intergenerational weighting can set `w_I = 0` and get a present-oriented welfare function.

**Why the theory does not require prioritarianism.** Because prioritarianism is a normative commitment, not a theoretical claim. The theory's job is to make the commitment explicit, not to select it.

### Two modes

The library supports two welfare modes:

- **Quadratic (default).** `W = Σ w_d · [γ_d · J - δ_d · (1 - J)²]`.
- **Linear floor.** `W = Σ w_d · γ_d · J - Σ w_d · δ_d · max(0, floor_d - J)`.

The linear floor mode is closer to classical sufficientarianism; the quadratic mode is closer to prioritarianism. The theory presents the quadratic form as its own, with the linear floor as an alternative for comparison.

### Takeaway

Welfare is a dimension-specific prioritarian function with three properties: monotone, convex in shortfall, separable across dimensions. The prioritarian commitment is explicit and swappable; the theory's job is to make it visible, not to select it.

# Part IV — Predictions and Interpretation

## Lesson 9: The eight hypotheses

The theory makes eight hypotheses. They are the theory's empirical content — the things that could be false. A theory that could not fail is not a theory; the hypotheses are what makes GSM-J a theory rather than a doctrine.

### H1 — Vertical alignment

**Claim.** Dimension-wise vertical alignment reduces cross-level justice shortfalls relative to aggregate alignment alone.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding that aggregate alignment produces lower shortfalls than dimension-wise alignment, controlling for other factors. Or a study finding no difference.

**How to test.** Compare two policy regimes: one that aligns jurisdictions on aggregate justice, another that aligns them dimension by dimension. Measure the cross-level shortfalls in each regime.

### H2 — Polycentric justice

**Claim.** Systems with multiple overlapping justice authorities outperform single-authority systems on recognition and intergenerational dimensions.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding single-authority systems achieve higher recognition or intergenerational scores. Or a study finding no difference.

**How to test.** Compare polycentric governance systems (e.g., federal systems with overlapping jurisdictions) against single-authority systems on recognition and intergenerational indicators.

### H3 — Non-exploitation

**Claim.** Jurisdictions subject to bounded non-exploitation constraints exhibit higher long-run flourishing than exploitation-permissive baselines, even when short-run aggregate welfare is lower.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding exploitation-permissive systems achieve higher long-run flourishing. Or a study finding no difference.

**How to test.** Compare systems with strong non-exploitation enforcement against those without. Measure long-run flourishing, not just aggregate welfare.

### H4 — Vulnerability sensitivity

**Claim.** Vulnerability-sensitive thresholds reduce justice floor violations among the worst-off jurisdictions without eliminating mutually beneficial exchange.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding vulnerability-sensitive thresholds eliminate mutually beneficial exchange. Or a study finding they do not reduce floor violations.

**How to test.** Compare uniform-threshold systems against vulnerability-sensitive systems. Measure both floor violations among the worst off and the volume of mutually beneficial exchange.

### H5 — Recursive learning

**Claim.** Systems with dimension-specific accountability learn justice-enhancing strategies faster than systems with aggregate accountability.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding aggregate accountability produces faster learning. Or a study finding no difference.

**How to test.** Compare systems with dimension-specific accountability mechanisms against those with aggregate mechanisms. Measure the rate of improvement in justice outcomes over time.

### H6 — Interaction matrix

**Claim.** The sign structure of `M_J` is empirically estimable and predicts which justice dimensions co-develop and which trade off. In component form: the contested pair `(I, P)` is the pair whose net sign the theory cannot predict; the eleven single-component pairs should recover the signs the theory proposes.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding that the matrix's sign structure is not estimable, or that single-component pairs fail to recover their proposed signs.

**How to test.** Estimate `M_J` from panel data under the paper's estimation protocol (Appendix D). Compare estimated signs to proposed signs. Report the contested pair separately from the single-component pairs.

### H7 — Frequency ceiling

**Claim.** Multi-channel implementation costs impose an endogenous ceiling on the frequency of aggressive justice interventions; systems that ignore this ceiling experience capability depletion.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding no ceiling on intervention frequency, or finding that frequency is unbounded.

**How to test.** Measure the depletion of cohesion and continuity following rapid interventions. Estimate the frequency at which depletion becomes significant.

### H8 — Recognition trap

**Claim.** Systems that improve distributive and procedural justice while neglecting recognition justice will exhibit declining social cohesion despite increasing aggregate welfare.

**What would falsify it.** A study finding recognition neglect does not produce cohesion decline. Or a study finding cohesion is unaffected by recognition neglect.

**How to test.** Compare systems that emphasize distributive and procedural gains against systems that also emphasize recognition. Measure both aggregate welfare and social cohesion.

### The pattern

Three hypotheses (H3, H6, H8) are the theory's central tests. They concern mechanisms, not totals. The theory's empirical content is concentrated in these three.

The other five (H1, H2, H4, H5, H7) are more general hypotheses about how the constraint architecture behaves. They are testable but less distinctive: a competing theory might also predict them.

### Takeaway

The theory makes eight hypotheses, all falsifiable. Three (H3, H6, H8) are the theory's distinctive tests, because they concern mechanisms the theory identifies as essential. The others are general hypotheses about constraint behavior.

## Lesson 10: What "conditional," "computational," and "middle-range" mean

Three labels attach to GSM-J, and each is doing a specific kind of work. Understanding what each label means is understanding what kind of theory GSM-J is.

### "Computational"

A **computational theory** is a set of claims about how a system works, expressed in a formal language precise enough to generate predictions and to be tested. It is not merely a **model** (a mathematical formalism) and not merely a **simulation** (an instrument for exploring a model). The paper's §1.6 makes this distinction explicitly: theory = claims, model = formalism, simulation = instrument.

**Why the distinction matters.** A paper that presents a model or a simulation without a theory can be asked: what does it claim about the world? A paper that presents a theory can be asked: is it true? The first question is about method; the second is about substance.

GSM-J claims that **justice-centered governance has a specific structure** — five dimensions, four constraints, three dynamics — and that this structure generates the eight hypotheses. The claims are the theory; the model and the simulation are how the claims are made precise and testable.

### "Conditional"

A **conditional theory** is a theory whose claims are conditional on the values of certain parameters. GSM-J is conditional in this sense: given specific values for the normative parameters (dimension weights, floors, prioritarian coefficients, tolerance thresholds, subsidiarity margins), the theory predicts specific consequences. Given different values, it predicts different consequences.

**Why the theory does not fix the parameters.** Because the theory cannot derive them. The theory is **normatively conditional**: it does not claim that a specific floor is correct, only that the floor must be specified and defended. This is what makes it a theory of justice *as a governance capability* rather than a particular doctrine of justice.

**What "conditional" does not mean.** It does not mean the theory is indeterminate. The theory makes precise claims about *how the system behaves* given any parameterization. What varies is the normative input; what is derived is the systemic consequence.

### "Middle-range"

A **middle-range theory** is a theory that explains a specific institutional phenomenon without claiming to explain all of social life. It is not a universal theory of justice (which would claim to explain what justice is); it is not a grand theory of everything (which would claim to explain all forms of human association); it is a theory of **how justice-centered governance behaves across interacting levels under specified parameters**.

**Why this matters.** A reader of the paper might ask: does GSM-J claim to explain what justice is? It does not. Does it adjudicate between comprehensive moral doctrines? It does not. Does it derive substantive principles from a founding premise? It does not. What it does is explain how a specific kind of system — multi-level governance with a justice-centered architecture — behaves.

§7.5 of the paper states this explicitly: "A theory that claimed to explain justice itself could not be tested; a middle-range theory that claims to explain justice-centered governance can be."

### Takeaway

GSM-J is a **computational** (formally represented, testable) **conditional** (parameterized by explicit normative commitments) **middle-range** (institutionally scoped) theory. Each label is doing specific work. Together they say what kind of theory GSM-J is and what it can and cannot do.

# Part V — Working with the Theory

## Lesson 11: A worked example

The paper's §6 illustrates the theory with a **stylized Nordic democracy**: three jurisdictions in a parent-child chain, high-capability but with moderate recognition and intergenerational shortfalls. The example is pedagogical, not empirical, and it is worth walking through carefully.

### The setup

Three jurisdictions:

- **National level** — high capabilities (accountability 0.85, competence 0.80, cohesion 0.82, continuity 0.78, learning 0.80), high distributive and procedural justice (0.78, 0.80), moderate recognition (0.62), corrective (0.45), intergenerational (0.55).
- **Province level** — capabilities and justice values somewhat lower.
- **Municipality level** — lower still.

Parent-child relationships connect national to province, province to municipality.

### What happens under default parameters

The system runs. Aggregate welfare rises modestly because the capabilities drift up and the justice cluster improves on the high-performing dimensions. But the C2 violations (floor shortfalls) persist: the recognition, corrective, and intergenerational dimensions remain below their floors at all levels. The C4 violations (vertical alignment) are systematic: the province falls below the national level in every dimension by more than the tolerance margin, and the municipality below the province.

The paper's §6.1 reports four findings:

1. Dimension-specific accountability reduces masked shortfalls in recognition and intergenerational justice.
2. Multi-channel intervention costs produce an endogenous frequency ceiling: aggressive intervention depletes cohesion and continuity after roughly 15–20 rapid interventions.
3. Symmetric vs. asymmetric `M_J` changes the long-run profile of justice dimensions without changing the direction of aggregate convergence.
4. The contested pair `(I, P)` produces net coefficients near zero under several parameterizations, and the resulting stability is an artifact of offsetting components, not of genuine independence.

### What the example shows

The example shows that **aggregate welfare and justice are not the same thing**. The system's aggregate welfare improves while recognition, corrective, and intergenerational justice remain below their floors. A theory that measured only aggregate welfare would miss the persistent shortfalls. A theory that used only bounded non-exploitation would miss them too, because no exploitation is occurring in those dimensions.

The example also shows the **frequency ceiling** (H7) and the **contested pair** (H6) in action. Aggressive intervention has costs; the contested pair's net coefficient near zero is consistent with offsetting components, and the theory predicts that no other mechanism explains it as well.

### What the example does not show

The example does not show that the theory is empirically correct. It uses illustrative profiles, not measurements. The findings are consistent with the theory; they do not test it. A test would require real data, real jurisdictions, and the estimation protocol of §3.3.3.

### Takeaway

The stylized-Nordic example is a pedagogical illustration, not a test. It shows how the theory's parts fit together and what the theory predicts. It does not show that the theory is empirically correct; that requires the estimation protocol and real data.

## Lesson 12: Common misunderstandings

Six misreadings of the theory, with corrections.

### "Isn't this just welfare economics with extra steps?"

No. Welfare economics takes a distribution of resources as given and evaluates it. GSM-J does not take a distribution as given: it asks whether the process that produced the distribution was just, in five separate dimensions. The five dimensions are not a refinement of utility; they are distinct kinds of harm that utility-based frameworks cannot see.

The recognition dimension is the clearest case. Recognition injustice leaves no measurable trace in resources or in utility. A welfare-economics framework that treated recognition as a preference would miss it entirely; GSM-J treats it as a dimension of justice that must be met on its own terms.

### "Why not just maximize aggregate justice?"

Because the aggregate can hide shortfalls. The theory's §3.3.1 is explicit: aggregation is for reporting only; the constraint architecture operates on the disaggregated vector. A jurisdiction can be "aggregately just" while failing in recognition, corrective, or intergenerational justice. The theory's constraints prevent that failure from being masked.

### "Aren't the five dimensions arbitrary?"

The dimensions are not arbitrary; each is derived from a specific kind of harm identified in the justice gap (Lesson 2). Drop any one and the theory cannot see the kind of harm that dimension is for. Add one and you have to specify what harm it catches that the five do not.

That said, the theory does not claim the five are the only possible decomposition. A different decomposition would be a different theory, and its adequacy would be tested by the same criteria: does it generate falsifiable hypotheses, and do those hypotheses survive empirical test?

### "Isn't this just Ostrom?"

No. Ostrom's work on polycentric governance provides one of the six traditions the theory draws on (§1.3), but the theory's core innovations — the justice gap, the five-dimensional capability, the constraint architecture — are not in Ostrom. The polycentric insight is important (multiple overlapping authorities tend to outperform single-authority systems) but it is not a theory of justice.

### "Doesn't this require a world government?"

No. The theory models six levels, including the global commons, but it does not require a single global authority. It requires that the constraint architecture be enforceable across levels, which can be achieved through treaty commitments, reciprocal agreements, or international institutions. Chapter IV of the theory addresses external conduct, and the theory's constitutional instrument (a separate deliverable) provides a national-level implementation.

### "Doesn't this require agreement on what justice is?"

No, and this is the point of the conditional status (Lesson 10). The theory requires agreement on the **form** of justice — five dimensions, four constraints, three dynamics — not on the specific values of the parameters. Different normative communities can specify different weights, floors, and thresholds and get different predictions from the same theory. What they cannot do is hide their normative commitments; the theory makes them explicit.

### Takeaway

The theory is not welfare economics, not aggregate maximization, not Ostrom, not a world-government proposal, and not a doctrine of substantive justice. Each of these misreadings comes from collapsing one of the theory's distinctions. The distinctions are the theory.

## Lesson 13: How to think with the theory

The theory is a tool, and like any tool it is best understood by using it. This final lesson describes the four ways the theory is designed to be used.

### As a diagnostic

The theory provides a vocabulary for asking where a governance system is failing. Not "is it just?" but "in which of the five dimensions, at which level, with what cross-level pattern?"

A policy analyst applying the theory to a specific context would ask:

- What are the distributive, procedural, recognition, corrective, and intergenerational justice levels at each level of government?
- Where do the floors (C2) get violated?
- Where does vulnerability (C3) trigger a reduced tolerance threshold?
- Where do child jurisdictions fall below their parents (C4)?
- Where is exploitation (C1) occurring, and is it adequately compensated?

These are concrete questions with concrete answers. The theory's diagnostic value is that it makes the answers comparable across contexts.

### As a design tool

The theory provides a template for institutional design. Not "here is a just constitution" but "here is what a justice-centered constitution must contain."

A constitutional designer applying the theory would ask:

- Are the five dimensions represented in the constitution?
- Is each dimension's floor specified, and if not, is there a mechanism for specifying it?
- Is vulnerability-sensitive protection built in?
- Is vertical alignment enforced dimension by dimension?
- Are the normative parameters explicit, contestable, and revisable?

The theory's design value is that it makes these requirements **explicit** rather than implicit in a design's structure.

### As a hypothesis generator

The theory provides eight hypotheses (Lesson 9). Each is a claim about how a justice-centered multi-level system behaves. Each is testable.

An empirical researcher applying the theory would ask:

- Can I test H3 (non-exploitation → higher long-run flourishing) with panel data?
- Can I estimate the interaction matrix `M_J` (H6) and see whether the contested pair produces a near-zero net?
- Can I measure whether recognition neglect produces cohesion decline (H8)?

The theory's empirical value is that it tells you what to look for and what would count as evidence against it.

### As a critique

The theory provides a standard for evaluating governance systems that are not justice-centered. Not "is it good or bad?" but "does it have the properties a justice-centered system would have?"

A critic applying the theory would ask:

- What forms of structural injustice does this system produce that its own metrics do not see?
- How does this system handle the recognition dimension, which its metrics may not measure?
- How does this system handle intergenerational justice, which its metrics may discount entirely?
- Where does this system rely on aggregate measures that mask shortfalls in a dimension?

The theory's critical value is that it gives you the vocabulary to name the failures that the system itself does not name.

### What the theory is not for

The theory is not a replacement for substantive ethical reasoning. It does not tell you what the right floor for distributive justice is, or how much weight recognition should have relative to distributive. Those are normative questions, and the theory requires you to answer them (explicitly) rather than offering to answer them for you.

The theory is not a prediction machine. It does not tell you what will happen in a specific case without parameterization and data. It tells you what *kind* of thing to expect, conditional on your parameterization.

The theory is not a policy prescription. It provides a framework for evaluating policies against justice criteria, but the choice of which policy to pursue is a political decision that the theory informs but does not determine.

### Takeaway

The theory is best used as a **diagnostic**, a **design tool**, a **hypothesis generator**, and a **critical vocabulary**. It is not a replacement for substantive ethical reasoning, not a prediction machine, and not a policy prescription. Its value is that it structures the questions you ask, not that it answers them for you.

## Summary of key ideas

For quick reference:

**The problem.** Multi-level governance needs a theory of justice that represents levels, treats justice as a capability, formalizes the conditions of legitimate cross-jurisdictional cost-imposition, and generates falsifiable hypotheses.

**The justice gap.** Bounded non-exploitation is necessary but insufficient for justice. The normal forms of structural injustice operate below the threshold of detectable extraction.

**The three innovations.** The justice gap, the five-dimensional justice capability, the constraint architecture. Each is necessary; none is sufficient.

**The five dimensions.** Distributive, procedural, recognition, corrective, intergenerational. Irreducible; each catches a distinct kind of harm.

**The four constraints.** Bounded non-exploitation, dimension floors, vulnerability sensitivity, vertical alignment. Each catches a failure the others miss.

**The interaction matrix.** Synergy and tension components, decomposed. The contested pair `(I, P)` is where the theory is least committal; the single-component pairs are where it is at risk.

**The dynamics.** Interaction, alignment pressure, exploitation drag. Co-evolution with the five capabilities. Dimension-specific accountability.

**The welfare function.** Dimension-specific, prioritarian by default, quadratic shortfall. Three properties: monotone, convex, separable.

**The eight hypotheses.** H3 (non-exploitation), H6 (interaction matrix), and H8 (recognition trap) are the theory's distinctive tests.

**The labels.** Computational (formal, testable), conditional (parameterized), middle-range (institutionally scoped). Each is doing specific work.

**The uses.** Diagnostic, design tool, hypothesis generator, critical vocabulary. Not substantive ethics, not prediction, not policy prescription.
